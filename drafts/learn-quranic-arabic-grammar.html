How to learn Quranic Arabic grammar

Start by telling the three kinds of word apart (Ism, Fi'l and Harf). Then learn what an Ism carries (its status, number, gender and type), and practice each new rule on a real Ayah the same day. A few minutes every day works better than a long session once a week.

Before you start: can you read the script?

Grammar lessons assume you can sound out Arabic letters with their vowel marks. If you can read the Quran aloud, even slowly, you are ready. If you can't yet, learn the alphabet first; any good Qaida or alphabet course will do. Aqal doesn't teach the alphabet or tajweed.

What to learn first

Arabic grammar has a lot of names in it, and beginners often stall because they meet them all at once. This order keeps each step small, and each step uses the one before it:

  1. The three kinds of word. Every word in the Quran is an Ism (it names or describes a person, place, thing or concept), a Fi'l (an action with time built in) or a Harf (it connects words and can't stand alone). Once you can sort an Ayah into these three, the rest has somewhere to attach.
  2. What an Ism carries. Each Ism has four properties: status, number (one, two, or more), gender and type (common or proper). You learn to read them from the word's shape and its ending.
  3. Status. The ending of an Ism shows the role it plays in the sentence. This is the part that changes meaning most often, and the part a word-for-word translation hides.
  4. Pronouns. He, you, I, we: who is speaking and who is spoken to. In the Quran this changes inside a single passage, so it pays to spot it quickly.
  5. Verbs and word patterns. How one root gives a family of related words. This comes after the Ism, because by then the endings and pronouns are familiar.

Practice on the Quran, not on made-up sentences

Most grammar books practice with sentences about a man going to the market. That works, but it means you learn the rule twice: once in the book and again when you meet it in an Ayah. Practicing on real Ayat from the first week means every rule you learn is one you have already seen in the Quran.

Here are the opening words of Al-'Ankabut 29:44, each word colored by its kind:

خَلَقَ اللَّهُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ بِ‍‍الْحَقِّ

"Allah created the heavens and the earth in truth." Al-'Ankabut 29:44

Fi'l · Ism · Harf

The first word is a Fi'l (an action: he created). Four are Ism (Allah, the heavens, the earth, the truth). Two are Harf, attached to the front of the next word: "and" and "in". Sorting a line like this is the first skill, and you can do it after a few short lessons.

How much time it takes

We can't give you an honest single number: it depends on how often you practice and how much Arabic you already know. What we can say is how to use the time well:

  • Short and daily beats long and rare. People remember material better when practice is spread over days than when it is crammed into one sitting (Cepeda et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2006).
  • Review words just before you would forget them. Aqal brings a word back after 3, 7, 21, 60 and 120 days.
  • Keep going back to Ayat you already know. Each new rule shows you something you missed the first time.

Common mistakes

  • Learning lists of grammar terms before seeing them used. Learn a name when you need it, then use it on an Ayah.
  • Learning only vocabulary. Word lists help, but without grammar you can't tell who did what to whom.
  • Waiting to "finish" grammar before reading the Quran. Read from the start; understanding grows line by line.
